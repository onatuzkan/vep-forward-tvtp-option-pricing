\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What a monthly strip identifies}

Calibration to a baseload strip fixes six linear functionals of the hourly
curve and, through Proposition~\ref{prop:centering}, the first moment of the
price at every horizon. It does not fix the intraday shape, the level over the
unquoted month, the dispersion around the curve or any premium, and the
largest single driver of option value is an input that no observed price
constrains. A reporting convention follows. In markets of this kind a value is
better stated conditional on an explicit anchor and accompanied by the range
that plausible alternatives induce; for the reference call the $\pm20\%$ band
gives $17.80$ to $528.96$\,\TRY{} around $166.75$\,\TRY, and the multi-date
evidence of Section~\ref{sec:strip} supports that width. The centring identity
is what makes this practical, because premia, anchors and residual parameters
can be varied without disturbing the calibration.

\subsection{What the strip predicts}

Whether forward prices are unbiased predictors of later spot prices is an old
question in power markets, framed by hedging pressure
\citep{BessembinderLemmon2002}, by risk premia whose sign depends on the
horizon \citep{BenthCarteaKiesel2008}, and by systematic forecast errors
\citep{Redl2009}. On Turkish monthly contracts the strip is neither unbiased
nor reliably biased in one direction: the pooled error averages $-18\%$ of the
forward level, yet two of seven dates reversed the sign. The out-of-sample
evaluation adds the hourly view, in which the forward curve carries all of the
bias of the predictive distribution. Separating a premium from a forecast error
would require either an instrument for expectations or a structural model of
the supply stack, and seven dates cannot identify either. For a book anchored
to this curve, position risk is dominated by the level it is anchored to: a
standard deviation of $775$\,\TRY{} in the level error compares with a
residual dispersion of about $530$\,\TRY{} at the horizons studied.

\subsection{Specification limits}

The main limit is the scaling of dispersion with the price level. A
fixed-scale transform combined with the delta mapping \eqref{eq:sigmamap}
makes the residual standard deviation proportional to the forward, and
Section~\ref{sec:variance} shows that this holds over 2023--2025 and fails in
2026, when relative dispersion rose by more than half. A scale that adapts to
the price regime, or a residual volatility that depends on the level
explicitly, is the natural extension. It cannot be added without care, because
the centring identity requires the first moment of the residual to remain
computable under the same measure.

The residual is additive and unbounded below while realised prices face
administrative limits. A specification with a slow second factor and explicit
price limits was developed alongside the present one, but clipping the state
distribution breaks the centring identity, so restoring
$\E^{\Q}[P_t]=F(t)$ would require a redesigned centring term. The alternative
of a multiplicative or logarithmic form respects the lower bound automatically
but loses forward consistency through Jensen's inequality, at a cost measured
in Section~\ref{sec:residual} in thousands of \TRY, whereas the simulated
probability of a negative price under the additive form is zero to four
decimal places.

Three parameters remain imperfectly documented. The transition intercepts are
reconstructions without standard errors, the scale $s_P$ is consistent with an
early estimation window that cannot be verified, and the regime labels were
reconciled from an output in which the high-variance state is the dominant one.
The independent estimation of Section~\ref{sec:estimation} bounds the
consequences: replacing all four transition coefficients by
maximum-likelihood estimates from the transformed price level changes the
reference call by $+3.75$\,\TRY, and the inherited volatilities
are retained because they reproduce the realised stationary variance, not
because they are individually correct.

\subsection{Scope of the evaluation}

The evaluation covers one market, one year of out-of-sample data and seven
forward-curve dates whose delivery windows partly overlap. Four of the seven
strips were published one to four days before their valuation date because
the date fell on a non-business day, and a strict same-day rule would leave
three dates. No option is traded on the underlying, so option values are
evaluated against realised payoffs rather than market prices, and no premium
can be calibrated. Extending the panel as quotes and outturns accumulate, and
calibrating the intensity premium if option or structured-contract prices
become observable, are the natural continuations.
