\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Option values and benchmarks}
\label{sec:surface}

At the reference contract, a call with $K=3{,}000$\,\TRY{} expiring 72 hours
after the last known hour, the forward is $F(T)=2{,}916.16$\,\TRY, the
residual standard deviation $532.26$\,\TRY{} and the call value
$166.75$\,\TRY. Because the residual saturates within a day, values change
little with maturity once the forward is fixed (Table~\ref{tab:values}), and
the simulated probability of a negative terminal price is zero to four
decimal places.

Three closed-form benchmarks are priced on the same forward and discount
factor: Black-76 and Bachelier with volatilities estimated from daily price
changes over 2019--2025, and the \citet{LuciaSchwartz2002} model with the
production $\kappa$ and the regime volatilities pooled at the stationary
occupancy. Near the money the Lucia--Schwartz value lies about 10\% above the
model, whereas the two models without mean reversion diverge with maturity,
reaching $+118\%$ (Bachelier) and $+195\%$ (Black-76) at 168 hours
(Figure~\ref{fig:values}a). Against payoffs realised in 2026 over a grid of 66
strike--maturity pairs, the mean absolute errors are $151$\,\TRY{} for the
model, $157$\,\TRY{} for Lucia--Schwartz, $272$\,\TRY{} for Bachelier and
$369$\,\TRY{} for Black-76. Expressed as Black-76 implied volatilities, the
model produces a smile that declines in strike at every maturity and a term
structure that falls from $3.29$ at 24 hours to $0.61$ at 720 hours at
$K=3{,}000$, the signature of a mean-reverting residual
(Figure~\ref{fig:values}b).

\subsection{Sensitivity to unidentified inputs}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} varies one unidentified input at a time and orders
the inputs by their effect on the reference call. The ordering is the main
result.

The forward level over the unquoted month dominates every other input by an
order of magnitude. Near the money, moneyness drives value, and a $\pm20\%$
shift moves the forward by about $580$\,\TRY{} against a residual dispersion of
$530$\,\TRY. Where the front month is unquoted, the precision of any reported
value is therefore bounded by the uncertainty in that level, and no refinement
of the residual layer can improve on the bound.

The transition-intensity premium is the second channel. Raising $\eta_{01}$
and lowering $\eta_{10}$ shifts occupancy toward the stress regime and raises
the call by up to $21.1\%$; the opposite combination lowers it by $30.3\%$
(Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}b), and with the drift premium added at its upper
bound the range widens to $-38.6\%$ and $+29.1\%$. The drift premium alone
reaches $+5.7\%$ at the bound of Section~\ref{sec:premia}, consistent with its
second-order effect. A premium on regime risk is therefore the one that option
data, if they became available, would identify most readily.

The residual-demand channel has a definite sign: shifting the covariate path
down by two standard deviations raises the call by $20.4\%$ and shifting it up
lowers it by $35.2\%$, because $\gamma_{01}<0$ makes high residual demand
suppress transitions into the high-variance regime. Low residual demand is the
condition in which renewable output displaces the flexible thermal units that
would otherwise absorb short-run imbalances, so the direction is plausible, but
it is reported as a property of the estimated coefficients. The second
covariate of the original estimation, the hourly change of residual demand,
alters the call by $-1.0\%$ when its estimated slopes are added with the mean
regime durations held fixed, so its omission from the production model is
immaterial at this maturity.

The remaining inputs are second order at 72 hours. Mean reversion at fixed
stationary variance was discussed in Section~\ref{sec:statvar}. The initial
regime distribution is forgotten within a few hours, since the regime memory
has a half-life of $1.37$ hours: replacing the filtered distribution by one
filtered under the re-estimated parameters moves the call by $20.8\%$ at
1 hour, $0.06\%$ at 24 hours and not at all at 72 hours. The irrelevance
belongs to the horizon, not to the input. The discount factor at 720 hours moves only from $0.988$ to $0.952$
across the range considered.

\subsection{Out-of-sample evaluation in 2026}
\label{sec:oos}

Predictive distributions were formed on 60 valuation dates, every third
business day from 5 January to 24 September 2026, under the timing rule of
Section~\ref{sec:timing}, with horizons of 6 to 72 hours after the last known
hour. On each date the forward curve was rebuilt from the latest quotes and
every parameter was held at its end-2025 value. The production parameters are
compared with the full-window re-estimate of Section~\ref{sec:estimation} and
with the three benchmarks, using the continuous ranked probability score
(CRPS), the probability integral transform (PIT), the tests of
\citet{Berkowitz2001} and \citet{DieboldMariano1995}, and interval coverage
\citep{DieboldGuntherTay1998,GneitingRaftery2007}.

The predictive mean equals the forward, so the error separates into a
forward-curve component and a residual component. Using the realised 2026
monthly level and month-specific hourly shape as a reference, the forward curve
exceeds realised prices by $385$ to $794$\,\TRY{} on average, with a
day-to-day standard deviation of $697$ to $843$\,\TRY{}
(Table~\ref{tab:oos}, Figure~\ref{fig:oos}a). The forward curve carries all of
the bias and about half of the error variance. The realised residual is $1.33$
to $1.55$ times wider than the model implies, part of which reflects the rise
in dispersion from 2025 to 2026 documented in Section~\ref{sec:variance}. PIT
values pile up near zero (Figure~\ref{fig:oos}b), as expected when realised
prices fall below a forward that overshoots, and 90\% intervals cover between
37\% and 68\% of outcomes.

The wider re-estimated set scores better on CRPS at four of five horizons, but
it prices options worse. Near the money, its mean pricing error against
realised payoffs is $101$ to $184$\,\TRY, against $13$ to $64$\,\TRY{} for the
production set, and its tail is too heavy. A good density score and an
unbiased option price are different targets here, because the dominant error
is in the level and a wider distribution transmits a level error more fully
into option value. The production set also outperforms the Lucia--Schwartz
benchmark on CRPS at four horizons ($p<0.001$), whereas Bachelier, whose
dispersion grows without bound, scores better at 48 and 72 hours. Across
moneyness, in-the-money calls are overpriced and out-of-the-money calls
underpriced, the pattern of a forward that is too high combined with a
residual that is too narrow.

Delta hedging with the monthly contract was evaluated on the same dates. The
sensitivity of the forward at the target hour to the monthly quote rises from
$0.09$ at 6 hours to $0.35$ at 72 hours, but the hedge earned exactly zero
because the reference price of the relevant contract did not change within any
evaluation window. Over 2022--2026 the daily reference price changes on 8.5\%
of contract-days; for contracts delivering in 2026 the share is 1.0\%, and on
quotation days in 2026 it is 0.6\%. The monthly strip neither spans hourly risk
nor moves often enough to hedge with.

\subsection{Stability of the variance agreement}
\label{sec:variance}

The agreement of Section~\ref{sec:statvar} was checked at seven earlier dates
and at the end of the evaluation sample. At each date the residual was
constructed from the preceding twelve months only, and its standard deviation
was compared with the production value implied by \eqref{eq:sigmamap} at the
twelve-month mean price $L$, which is close to $0.199\,L$. From mid-2023 to the
end of 2025 the ratio of realised to implied dispersion lies between $0.93$ and
$1.13$, with a mean of $1.02$ (Figure~\ref{fig:variance}a). It is $1.26$ for
2022, a year of extreme prices, $1.65$ for the twelve months to September 2026
and $1.91$ for 2026 alone; a pooled hourly shape raises every ratio by 9 to
18\%.

The 2026 departure is not a level effect, since the implied dispersion is
evaluated at each window's own mean price. Realised dispersion relative to the
price level rises from $0.19$--$0.23$ over 2023--2025 to $0.33$ and $0.38$ in
the two 2026 windows (Figure~\ref{fig:variance}b).
Hours below $50$\,\TRY{} rise from under 1\% to 5--7\% of the sample in 2026,
but they occur at midday, are largely absorbed by the hourly shape, and
excluding them leaves the ratios almost unchanged. The scaling built into a
fixed-scale transform with delta mapping, dispersion proportional to the price
level, therefore holds over 2023--2025, is exceeded in 2022 and fails in 2026.

\subsection{Predictive error of the monthly strip}
\label{sec:strip}

Because the curve reproduces the quotes exactly, any error in the level is
inherited from the strip. Against realised prices from January to July 2026,
5{,}088 hours, the December 2025 strip exceeded realised monthly means by
$1{,}019$\,\TRY{} on average, by $24\%$ in July and $76\%$ in May
(Figure~\ref{fig:strip}a). In May, 222 of 744 hours cleared below
$10$\,\TRY. The period coincided with strong hydrological conditions and record
renewable output \citep{SirinYilmaz2020}, and the deviation is described rather
than attributed. January, whose level came from the anchor, shows a bias of
$-14.47$\,\TRY, which reflects the information in a spot observed one hour
before the window opened rather than any merit of the interpolation rule.

The curve construction contains no historical parameter and can be replayed at
earlier dates without look-ahead. At seven dates from December 2022 to December
2025, 49 date--month pairs, realised prices fell below the strip at five dates
and above it at two, with date means between $-1{,}465$ and $+290$\,\TRY{}
(Figure~\ref{fig:strip}b). The pooled mean error is $-603$\,\TRY, or $-18.2\%$,
with a standard deviation of $775$\,\TRY, and the 2026 outcome lies at the
33rd percentile of the pooled distribution. Aggregated to one observation per
date the mean gives $t=-2.29$ ($p=0.062$), and windows that do not overlap give
$p$ between $0.11$ and $0.49$, so the strip is described as a biased and
sign-unstable predictor without a calibrated significance level. Across the
seven anchored months the realised relative error of the anchor has a standard
deviation of $11.7\%$, close to the $11.5\%$ of a uniform $\pm20\%$ band, and a
mean of $-3.5\%$; the band used in Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} has the right
width but is not a containment bound.
